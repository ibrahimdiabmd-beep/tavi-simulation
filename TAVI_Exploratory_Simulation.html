We did not run it once. We ran it 10,000 times.

Two inputs are uncertain: the continuous annual mortality hazard μ is reported with a 0.12–0.60 interval, and the excess queue with a 4,020–5,959 interval. The central μ=0.36 is a hazard, corresponding to a one-year mortality probability of 1−e−0.36=30.2%, not 36%.

Following the source paper's resampling approach, the model is re-run 10,000 times with a fixed, recorded seed, drawing both inputs from normal distributions reconstructed from those intervals. Draws are not clipped to the reported interval limits, but any non-positive backlog or mortality-hazard draw is rejected and redrawn because neither parameter can be negative. The output reports the mean and 2.5th/97.5th percentiles — named Monte Carlo, after the casino, because every run is a roll of the dice. It is running live on this page: every bar below is real runs of the real engine, not a recording. The tool also offers a 100,000-draw convergence check and a positive, moment-matched lognormal sensitivity analysis; both preserve the Scenario B advantage, while the tail intervals change with distribution choice.

Recorded TAVI activity varies substantially across England, and prolonged waits can expose patients to harm. This tool asks a bounded question: under a historical waiting-list model, how do outcomes change when explicit operational assumptions are varied?

The idea in one paragraph

There is a queue of patients waiting for treatment. Every year, some are treated and leave the queue. Some die before they are treated. The dissertation examines four plausible operational determinants. This tool represents them as explicit scenario levers and shows the conditional outputs of the model.

Each of the four has its own page. Change what you like, then open Results.

How certain is this?

The uncertain inputs are the continuous annual waiting-list mortality hazard μ (central value 0.36; reported interval 0.12–0.60) and the excess queue W₀ (4,989; interval 4,020–5,959). The source-style analysis reconstructs normal distributions from those intervals, rejects and redraws non-positive values, and takes 10,000 positive-support draws. It reports the mean plus 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. μ=0.36 is a continuous hazard and implies a 30.2% one-year death probability. A fixed seed (20260816) makes the numerical experiment reproducible.

One honest boundary: this tests input uncertainty only. If the model's structure itself is wrong, Monte Carlo will not reveal it — that is why the separate implementation-fidelity verification is reported.

How does the assumed throughput loss change the result?

Because no TAVI-specific estimate was identified, the tool displays the model's structural response across the tested 0–60% range rather than defending the default as an empirical value.

Which input moves the answer most? (Figure 4.3 in the dissertation)

One-at-a-time sensitivity around Scenario A (+20% capacity, 40% switched, ρ=0.275): each parameter is swung across a stated range while the others stay fixed, and the swing in clearance time is recorded. The ranges are printed with each bar, because a swing means nothing without its range.

What is the procedure-only outlay?

This deliberately limited arithmetic multiplies the number treated before clearance by £26,145. It does not treat operator caseload guidance as a staffing-productivity rule, and it does not estimate consultant FTEs. It also omits the TAVI/SAVR cost mix, avoided surgery, labs, beds, nursing, devices, capital, quality-adjusted life-years and discounting. It is therefore neither a budget-impact analysis nor a cost-effectiveness analysis.

Structural diagnostic: why this cannot rank policies economically

The grid search is retained only as a diagnostic. In this simplified engine, all three levers affect outcomes through realised extra capacity Te, while the partial outlay is a fixed price per modelled treatment. Equal Te therefore gives equal clinical outputs and equal partial outlay by construction. The grid is not evidence that real policies have equal costs.

2. Can the remaining profiles rank the two systems? No. The England/UK set has 13 populated indicators and Germany 6, drawn from different years and sometimes different definitions. The maths

Every equation used anywhere in this tool, where it comes from, and why it was chosen.

The deterministic waiting-list equations are taken from a published, peer-reviewed paper. The four-domain score, its indicator selection, anchors, missing-data handling and policy interpretation are dissertation constructions. Part A — Diagnostic policy-and-capacity composite

A1. Putting different units on the same scale

score = 1 + 4 × (value − worst) / (best − worst)

Why. The indicators are measured in incompatible units: procedures per million, days, percentages, pounds per quality-adjusted life year. They cannot be averaged as they stand. This converts each one to a common 1–5 scale, where 1 is the worst plausible value and 5 the best. Indicators where a lower number is better, such as waiting days, are inverted so that 5 always means good.

Source. Standard min-max normalisation, as set out in the OECD handbook on building composite indicators (Nardo et al., 2008).

A2. Combining indicators within one dimension

dimension score = (sum of its indicator scores) ÷ (number of indicators available)

Why. A plain average is used inside a dimension because the indicators there measure the same underlying thing in different ways. Dividing by the number available, rather than the number possible, is what allows a country with missing data to be scored at all. It is also the source of the weakness flagged on the previous page.

Source. Nardo et al. (2008).

A3. Combining four available constructed domains into one number

composite = (D₁ × D₂ × D₃ × D₄)1/4

Why this and not a plain average. A geometric mean reduces full compensation between domains. Version 12 calculates this secondary summary only for the England/UK evidence set, where all four domain scores are available. No German total is calculated because commissioning evidence is missing. This is a modelling choice, not proof that the domains are valid or exact bottlenecks.

Sources and adaptation. Penchansky and Thomas (1981) motivate thinking about multiple dimensions of access, but their original framework has five dimensions and differs from this four-domain operational adaptation. Geometric aggregation is supported as a composite-indicator technique by Nardo et al. (2008) and discussed in the HDI context by Klugman, Rodríguez and Choi (2011).

A4. Sensitivity and comparability boundary

Shared-indicator check = four indicators across guidance and geography only

Why. Arithmetic and user-weighted alternatives can test the England/UK construction, but they cannot create missing German commissioning or comparable capacity evidence. The four shared indicators do not reproduce the four-domain construct and are therefore descriptive only.

Source. Sensitivity testing of composite indices is recommended practice (Saisana, Saltelli and Tarantola, 2005); the decision not to calculate a German four-domain total is a missing-data safeguard used in this dissertation.

Part B — Modelling the queue (the simulation)

B1. How the queue changes over time

dW/dt = f − rT − rS − μW

In words. The change in the queue equals new patients arriving, minus those treated by TAVI, minus those treated by surgery, minus those who die while waiting.

Meaning of μ. μ is a continuous annual mortality hazard. At μ=0.36, the implied one-year death probability is 1−e−0.36=30.2%; describing it as “36% die within a year” would be incorrect.

Why. This is the standard way to describe a stock that fills and drains at the same time. It is the minimum structure needed to capture the fact that waiting itself kills people, which a simple "capacity versus demand" calculation misses entirely.

Source. Stickels et al. (2022), BMJ Open.

B2. Extra capacity

Te = rT + rS − f

Why. Only capacity above the rate needed to keep pace with new arrivals actually shortens the queue. Capacity below that line merely stops it growing. Te is the surplus, and it is the single quantity all four pillars act upon.

Source. Stickels et al. (2022).

B3. Time to clear the queue

tc = ln(1 + μW₀ / Te) ÷ μ

Why a logarithm appears. This is the exact solution of equation B1, not an approximation. The logarithm is the reason the model shows diminishing returns: each extra unit of capacity clears the queue by a smaller amount than the one before. Within the option set examined, that feature contributes to diminishing returns; it does not establish a universal cost ranking.

Source. Stickels et al. (2022), derived in their supplementary material.

B4. How many die waiting

m(tc) = W₀ − Te × tc

Why. Everyone in the queue leaves it one of two ways: treated, or dead. The second term counts those treated. Whatever is left is the first group.

Source. Stickels et al. (2022).

B5. The shape of the queue over time (the chart on the Results page)

W(t) = (W₀ + Te/μ) × e−μt − Te/μ

Why. Equation B3 gives only the end point. This gives the whole path, so the queue can be drawn shrinking rather than reported as a single number. It is the same equation solved for W instead of t.

Source. Standard solution of equation B1.

Part C — What this dissertation added

C1. Capacity created by the two levers together

Te = cap × (rS0 + rT0) + conv × rS0 × (1 + cap) × (ratio − 1)

Why the second term looks complicated. Because the two levers interact. If you first grow the service by 20%, there are then more surgical patients available to switch. So the switching lever acts on the enlarged pool, not the original one, which is what "(1 + cap)" represents. Adding the two effects separately would understate their combined value.

The "ratio" is 3:2, meaning three TAVI procedures fit in the time of two surgeries. Only the difference between them, "(ratio − 1)", is new capacity.

Source. Reproduced from the worked example in Stickels et al.'s (2022) supplementary material. An earlier version of this tool added the two terms separately, which understated the interaction and is now corrected.

C2. Throughput-realisation loss this dissertation's extension

Te realised = Te theoretical × (1 − ρ)

Why. The published model applies the capacity it creates. This extension instead tests a proportional loss between theoretical and realised extra throughput. It does not assign that loss to Blueteq or any other mechanism.

Honest caveat. No TAVI-specific estimate was identified in this review. The Results page therefore reports the structural response across the tested 0–60% range.

C3. Splitting England into three groups of areas this dissertation's extension

share of new capacity = activity-weighted area share   or   area-count share

Why two versions. The first allocates by representative activity. The second allocates both backlog and capacity by the number of former CCG areas, implicitly treating all areas as equal in population and need. Equal clearance times under the second rule are therefore an algebraic consequence, not a population-based empirical result.

Source and limitation. Tier rates are illustrative summaries derived from access bands in Aktaa et al. (2024). Real population, age and clinical-need weights are not included.

C4. Turning a published confidence interval into a spread of values

standard deviation = (upper limit − lower limit) ÷ (2 × 1.96)

Why. This reconstructs a normal-distribution standard deviation from a reported 95% interval. The Version 12 primary Monte Carlo retains the reconstructed normal distributions but rejects and redraws any non-positive backlog or mortality-hazard value, runs 10,000 simulations, and reports the mean with the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. A 100,000-draw convergence check tests numerical stability. A second 100,000-draw sensitivity analysis uses positive lognormal inputs matched to the same mean and standard deviation, testing whether conclusions depend on the normal shape.

Source. Standard statistical conversion and the resampling approach described by Stickels et al. (2022), with positive-support rejection sampling added because neither W₀ nor μ can be negative.

C5. Procedure-only partial arithmetic not an economic evaluation

partial procedure outlay = (W₀ − modelled deaths) × £26,145

Why this was narrowed. The professional-society threshold of 100 procedures per operator is an experience/caseload standard, not evidence that one additional consultant creates 100 procedures per year. Version 12 retains the removal of consultant-equivalent counts and the £161,600 earnings proxy.

Boundary. The remaining multiplication is descriptive only. It treats the modelled treated total at a single procedure price and omits TAVI/SAVR mix, avoided surgery, staff, labs, beds, nursing, devices, capital, QALYs, discounting and a common analytic horizon. It must not be used to rank policies, claim cost-effectiveness or claim conservatism.

Is it accurate? — implementation-fidelity verification

Not external clinical validation. The deterministic engine is checked against all seven published scenarios and both reported endpoints: clearance time and deaths while waiting.

The source is Stickels et al. (2022), published in BMJ Open. They built a model of the aortic stenosis waiting list after COVID-19. This tool implements their deterministic equations, then adds explicitly labelled dissertation extensions. The table compares point estimates and relative deviations for all seven scenarios. Agreement here checks implementation fidelity; it does not validate the model against independent contemporary patients.

What the equations actually say

dW/dt = f − rT − rS − μW. The queue grows as new patients arrive (f), and shrinks as patients are treated by TAVI (rT) or surgery (rS), or die while waiting (μ).

tc = ln(1 + μW₀ / Te) / μ. The time to clear the queue, where W₀ is how many are waiting at the start and Te is the extra capacity created.

m(tc) = W₀ − Te · tc. Everyone in the queue is either treated or dies; this counts the second group.

The logarithm is why doubling the money does not halve the deaths. Each extra unit of capacity helps slightly less than the one before.

What this tool cannot tell you

The number waiting is a figure from November 2020. No newer published figure exists, so every result describes clearing a queue of that size, not today's queue.

Everyone in the queue is treated as clinically identical. Real patients differ in urgency and risk.

Reproducing the published results supports faithful implementation of the deterministic equations. It does not prove the underlying model is right, or that it still applies in 2026.

The procedure-only outlay omits major resource categories and contains no quality-of-life adjustment, common horizon or discounting. It cannot rank policies or satisfy a formal budget-impact or health-economic assessment.
